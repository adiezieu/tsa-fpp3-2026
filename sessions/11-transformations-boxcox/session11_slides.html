    The problem  ·  Session 5's question, with a consequence

    When the swings grow with the level, the series belongs on another scale.

    • You have seen this twice. Session 5 called it a multiplicative series. Session 9 called it a failure of property 3: the residual spread drifts, so one interval width cannot be honest.
    • The fix is a change of scale, not a change of model. On the log scale a 10% swing is the same height whether the series sits at 100 or at 10,000.
    • Today makes the choice systematic. Session 5 picked a rung of the ladder by eye; today a number, lambda, picks it for you, and you decide whether to trust it.

    The plan is to transform the series, fit an additive model, and then undo the transformation, which is what Session 12 covers.

    Why "another scale" rather than "another model": every model in this course describes additive structure, a level plus a trend plus a seasonal swing of fixed size. A multiplicative series breaks that assumption, and the cheapest way to restore it is to measure the series on a scale where multiplication becomes addition. That is exactly what a logarithm does.

    The dashed lines on the left panel are a quick visual test: if the envelope of the series opens like a funnel, the spread tracks the level.

    Logarithms  ·  the one property you need

    A logarithm turns multiplication into addition.

    • Equal distances are equal percentages. On a log scale, going from 10 to 20 is the same step as going from 1,000 to 2,000: both are "doubled". That is why the swings on the previous slide became the same size.
    • log() in R is the natural logarithm, base e. The base only changes the ruler's units; the property is the same.
    • A log straightens an exponential trend. Growth of a constant percentage per year is a straight line on a log scale, which is the other reason to transform.

    If logarithmic scales are new to you, read 11_B in this folder before Session 12. It is fifteen pages of pictures.

    Two identities worth being able to derive, from the notebook: a^x = e^(x ln a) and the change of base log_a(x) = log_b(x) / log_b(a). Neither is examined, but both are what make "the base only changes the units" true.

    A small change on the log scale is approximately a percentage change on the original scale: a rise of 0.05 in log(y) is a rise of about 5% in y. The approximation is good for changes up to about 10%; 11_B shows why.

    The log of zero  ·  and the patch for it

    A series with zeros cannot be logged. Add one first, and zeros stay zeros.

    • The logarithm is only defined for positive numbers. One zero in the series, a closed shop on a holiday, and log() returns minus infinity for that day.
    • Why add exactly 1. Zeros map to zeros, and for small values the transformation barely moves them: near 0, log(1 + x) is about x. Any other constant would break both properties.
    • Use log1p() in R. It computes log(1 + x) accurately for tiny x, where the obvious formula loses precision.

    Negative values are a different problem, and no power transformation fixes them cleanly. The notebook links to the discussion; it is outside this course.

    The notebook asks why 1 and not some other constant k. With k = 1 the point (0, 0) is on the curve and the tangent there has slope 1, so the transformation is the identity for small values. With any other k, a zero would map to log(k), which is not zero, and the slope at zero would be 1/k.

    This patch is also what Session 19 needs for multiplicative-error models, which are numerically unstable when the series has zeros.

    fpp3 3.1  ·  one family, one dial

    Box–Cox is Session 5's ladder with a number on each rung.

    wt = log(yt) if λ = 0 wt = (sign(yt) |yt|λ − 1) / λ otherwise
    • One parameter, lambda, chooses the rung. The lower the lambda, the stronger the transformation. Lambda 1 shifts the series down by 1 and changes nothing else.
    • The log is the zero rung and the natural reference point: it is the only rung whose result you can still read in percentages.
    • The "−1 over lambda" part is bookkeeping. It makes the family change smoothly as lambda passes through zero, so that lambda 0.05 and lambda 0 give almost the same picture.

    The dial can be set anywhere between these marks, so the next slide shows how R chooses a setting.

    Variable versus parameter, from the notebook: y_t is the variable, the thing the function acts on; lambda is the parameter, fixed before the function is applied. Each value of lambda is a different member of the family.

    Why the formula has sign(y) and |y|: it lets the power version of the transformation run on negative values. The log version still cannot, which is why the previous slide's patch exists. Transformations with lambda far below zero make the prediction intervals of Session 12 very wide; in practice lambda rarely sits below about 0.
    11_A › Guerrero feature

    Choosing lambda  ·  features(y, guerrero)

    Guerrero picks the lambda that stops each year's spread depending on that year's level.

    lambda <- aus_production |> features(Gas, features = guerrero) |> pull(lambda_guerrero) # 0.11
    • The idea. Cut the series into seasons, measure the level and the spread of each one, and choose the lambda for which the spread is as unrelated to the level as possible.
    • Treat the output as a suggestion and check it against the plot. For Australian gas it says 0.11: close to zero, so a plain log does almost the same job and keeps the units readable.
    • Always plot the result. The number tells you the swings ought to be even now, and only the plot shows whether they actually are.

    Guerrero's lambda is a helpful number, but it should not be trusted on its own.

    The method is from Guerrero (1993). It splits the series into blocks of one seasonal period, computes the mean and the standard deviation of each block, and chooses the lambda that makes the ratio of standard deviation to mean, raised to a power that depends on lambda, as constant as possible across blocks. The picture on the slide is the intuition; the formula is not examined.

    features() is the same carrier as ljung_box in Session 9: one function, many features, each returning a row of numbers. pull() takes the one column you want out of that row.
    11_A › Usual workflow

    The decision  ·  three outcomes

    If lambda is near 1, do nothing; near 0, take the log; otherwise use Box–Cox, and then look at the result.

    • A lambda near 1 means the transformation is not worth it. The series would barely change and you would lose the ability to read the numbers.
    • A lambda near 0 means you should take the log instead. It gives nearly the same picture, and a log can be explained to anyone because changes become percentages.
    • In between, use the dial. In all three cases, the final decision rests on the plot.

    When two transformations do about equally well, prefer the one you can explain.

    "Close to" is a judgment, not a threshold. In the worked examples, 0.93 counts as close to 1 and both 0.08 and minus 0.04 count as close to 0, while 0.28 and 0.61 are in between. The final judge, as the notebook says, is the performance of the model fitted afterwards, which Sessions 13 and 14 measure.

    The last box matters more than it looks. A transformation chosen by a number and never plotted is the most common way this goes wrong in homework, and the second example in the homework is built to catch it.
    11_A › Caveat

    The caveat  ·  Canadian gas production

    Box–Cox only fixes a spread that follows the level.

    • Every rung of the dial makes the same assumption: bigger level, bigger swings, in proportion. Shrink the big values and the big swings shrink with them.
    • Canadian gas breaks it. The swings are large in the middle years, at a middling level, and small at both ends. Shrinking the large values does nothing to the middle.
    • You can only see this in the plot. Guerrero will still return a number, but the envelope of the series is not a funnel, so the number does not mean what it usually means.

    A transformation only helps when the spread is proportional to the level, so check that this is the problem before you apply one.

    What would help with Canadian gas is outside this course: a model whose seasonal component is allowed to change size over time, which is what STL's season window from Session 7 allows, or regression with time-varying effects in the follow-up course.

    Two of the four worked examples in the notebook are versions of this caveat: tobacco, whose spread stays large when the level falls, and the bulls series, whose changing spread is not clearly multiplicative.

    11_A › Some notes on transformations

    What a transformation costs

    A transformation is only worth making if it helps the model.

    Interpretability

    A forecast of 4.2 log-dollars means nothing to anyone. A log at least keeps changes readable as percentages, which other values of lambda do not.

    Prediction intervals

    Transformations change the intervals a lot, and a low lambda can make them enormous. Session 12 shows how they come back to the original scale, and why they stop being symmetric.

    The way back

    Every transformed forecast must be undone to be useful. Declare the transformation inside the model, and fable undoes it for you. Session 12 again.

    Often: nothing

    Many series forecast perfectly well untransformed. The default is to leave the data alone until the plot, or Session 9's residuals, say otherwise.

    • The final judge is the model's performance on data it has not seen, which Sessions 13 and 14 measure.

    A log you can explain beats a lambda you cannot, and no transformation beats both when the spread is already even.

    A caution for Group Assignment 2 and for the exam: "the Guerrero feature suggested it" is not a justification. The justification is the plot before, the plot after, and what the residuals look like once a model is fitted.

    Two things change when a model is fitted to a transformed series, and Session 12 covers both: the point forecast is no longer the mean of the forecast distribution on the original scale, and the interval is no longer symmetric around it.
